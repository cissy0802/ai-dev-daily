Measurement & Evidence

How do you tell whether AI actually raised output, rather than just usage and code volume?

22 sources · updated 2026-11-18 · BigCat

// What we can learn so far

  1. Code volume overstates impact; this is the most important disagreement in this category. Labs self-report code metrics: Anthropic says a typical engineer merges 8x as much code per day as in 2024 (by lines, which the authors admit overstates productivity), and Google's earnings call cites 35% more code shipped with no baseline. Independent research paints a different picture: the Harvard/Jellyfish study of 655 companies saw agents raise lines of code 44%, but issue completion only 13.7% (barely significant), while review wait time rose 56.1%. These numbers use different bases and can't be stitched into one trend line. The firm conclusion is that you must also measure final delivery (issues/epics, customer-facing changes). (multiple orgs)
  2. Self-report surveys reflect perception only and aren't productivity evidence. The Anthropic employee survey shows time savings far above the average US worker's, but the sample is self-selected and employees hold equity; Anthropic's researchers self-report "4x output," and the authors themselves cite METR that self-reports overestimate; Microsoft's "88% of developers report higher throughput" gives no sample size. Use surveys to gauge depth of use; output needs behavioral data. (multiple orgs)
  3. Usage isn't value; set a baseline before launch. OpenAI's per-department token shares measure tool choice, not output. Microsoft Digital requires expected value and baseline to be written down before launch with monthly reviews, says explicitly that "a tool can be used frequently without improving the process," and tracks where saved time goes. Costs need managing too: after Uber used up its full-year budget in four months, it set a $1,500 monthly cap per person per coding tool. (multiple orgs)
  4. Measure the bottleneck stages specifically, especially review and CI. The Harvard study found time from review request to approval rose after agent adoption; Anthropic itself calls human review the new Amdahl bottleneck; with test volume up about 4x, Linear held wait times only by treating "time PRs wait for CI" and "runner time per test" as first-class metrics. (multiple orgs)
  5. To measure agent reliability, look at intervention rates and quality proxies, not just success counts. OpenAI reports by task-duration bucket: over half of successful 4-8 hour tasks needed human intervention. Anthropic compares deletion/revert rates of agent code and human code from the same person, month, and directory. Anthropic uses a "task basket + automation level" to measure how much work AI does, and validates its grader with blind ratings (grader-human agreement was only 59%). Meta ads technique propagation measures agents per stage: recall for candidate selection, and human interventions per experiment plus the share of issues agents fix on their own for execution. (multiple orgs)
  6. Run controlled comparisons on your own codebase before rolling out a model or skill. Databricks built a private benchmark from its own PRs and found that models cheaper per token were more expensive per task, and that switching harnesses could change cost by 2x or more. Dan Luu's experiments showed that neither TDD prompting nor highly starred testing skills noticeably improved correctness. Practices that "sound right" need testing first. (multiple orgs)
  7. Numbers published by vendors and platforms are directional signals only. GitHub Octoverse's "over 40% of PRs initiated by agents" depends on detectable signals, and merge rate isn't quality; Microsoft Customer Zero's hours saved come with no calculation method; Google's earnings call doesn't define "errors." Before benchmarking against them, align their denominators and definitions with yours. (vendor self-reported)

A Harvard study of 655 companies finds AI agents sharply increase code volume but issue completion much less, with the bottleneck in code review.

Harvard2026-11-09 · Artificial Intelligence in the Firm: Bottlenecks in Software Production
What to borrow: The most important counterweight / calibration evidence in this batch: looking only at LOC, commits, and PRs overstates AI's effect. Also track issue/epic completion, PR review wait time, share of changes requested, and share of reviewer effort. Organizations that push agents without also rebuilding review (risk tiering, automated verification, AI pre-review) will see output stall in review.
What they did · verbatim numbers · caveats

Two Harvard economists used proprietary data from the engineering analytics platform Jellyfish (445 million work events across GitHub, Jira, and calendars; 655 companies, 720,000 employees) and ran a difference-in-differences analysis on staggered company adoption of AI coding assistants/agents. Findings: AI greatly increases code output, but final software output such as Jira issues/epics rises much less, with no detectable effect on employment. The bottleneck shifts to code review: time waiting for review, review duration, and change requests all rise. 79.1% of companies already use AI review tools, but review remains mostly human.

  • AI assistants lead to moderate increases in code output — 12 months after adoption, firms exhibit an increase of 30.4% in lines of code, 16.8% in commits, and 20.9% in pull requests. AI agents lead to larger additional increases in all three measures — 44.0% for lines of code, 36.0% for commits, and 35.7% for pull requests opened.
  • The effects from AI assistants are near zero, at −1.5% on issues and 3.4% on epics. The effects from AI agents are slightly larger — 13.7% on issues and 18.0% on epics — with the effect of agents on issues resolved being borderline significant.
  • We find evidence of a bottleneck in this stage: after adoption of AI agents, the average time from the code’s review submission to its approval increases by 56.1%. This change represents both an increase in the time that code waits in a “backlog,” by 59.9%, and an increase in the time after code has begun active review, by 58.5%.
  • This bottleneck is not eliminated by adoption of AI tools in the review process — 79.1% of firms have adopted AI code review tools, but still largely keep humans in the loop, with the overwhelming majority of reviews and comments being written by humans.
Basis: The sample is 655 software companies using Jellyfish (self-selected: companies willing to buy an engineering metrics platform), with data through 2026-03; adoption timing is partly inferred from GitHub activity. The paper's 11-09 version has different numbers from the Ars report of 10-09 (based on an older version: LOC +30%, review time +49%); the current paper version takes precedence.
Caveats: Observational, not randomized; data ends 2026-03, before several stronger models. The authors' job market paper, and the long-term forecasting model rests on strong assumptions. HN discussion and Ars reader comments largely echo "forced to use AI, drowning in review."
