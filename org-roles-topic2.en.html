Org & Roles

Once AI-native, how should jobs, levels, management, and team structure change?

20 sources · updated 2026-11-12 · BigCat

// What we can learn so far

  1. Anchor leveling and performance criteria on outcomes, not AI usage. The Amazon memo states plainly that once token usage or code-change counts become targets, they lead to tokenmaxxing; it treats them only as diagnostics and uses P10/P90 to find teams that aren't using AI well. The former Meta engineer's account is the counterexample: "90% AI code" became a performance floor and produced shallow tests and fragile code. (multiple orgs)
  2. Managers need to use it hands-on first. Amazon requires technical and management leaders to have used the tools in production work and to be able to say where they don't help; Anthropic's engineering director lists "managers work as ICs first" as a non-negotiable principle; FurtherAI requires EMs to use the product in staging themselves. For people who manage managers, a starting point is the GitHub director's approach: scheduled automations take over meta-work like meeting prep and commitment tracking, while recognition and feedback are still written personally. (multiple orgs)
  3. Pushing tools only raises individual speed; team throughput requires changing the process. Microsoft Digital calls this a "hard lesson": every person-to-person handoff loses intent, so it moved to a team constitution plus spec-driven development, with leadership making it the standard. FurtherAI retooled one delivery stage per month and measured both "code changes" and "customer-facing changes"; the latter grew less and later. (multiple orgs)
  4. The bottleneck shifts from writing code to human attention and decisions. Anthropic's internal research found about 80% of time in interactive sessions is spent waiting on humans, and meta-work such as writing specs and monitoring agents has doubled as a share of work. FurtherAI chose to push planning and review down to engineers rather than add management layers. A critical vulnerability at the PHP Foundation got stuck on consensus decision-making, which was later changed to majority vote. Org design has to answer "who decides, and how fast." (multiple orgs)
  5. Role boundaries are blurring and need to be redrawn deliberately: PMs use prototypes instead of long docs (Microsoft Foundry Labs), design became the new bottleneck once engineering sped up (Anthropic designer), embedded tools PMs are appearing on the business front line (Anthropic GTM architect), and whole teams have been redeployed to prompts and evals (Anthropic). For hiring and leveling, see FurtherAI's "how big a problem can you own end to end" and OpenAI FDE's "learning ability matters more than industry experience." (multiple orgs)
  6. Treating agents as teammates needs explicit norms. Anthropic requires decisions to land in public channels agents can see, humans and agents to share one roster, and autonomy to expand by task type. The Claude Code team extended automated review area by area over six months or more, and core code is still reviewed by code owners. Note that Anthropic's "public by default" data boundaries are looser than at most large companies; change your data classification policy before copying this. (single org)
  7. Once individual experimentation is encouraged, it soon needs to be consolidated onto a platform. The Microsoft PM team saw several people building similar agents and shifted to promoting reusable skills. OpenAI FDE consolidated custom apps into plugins on a common harness, deleting about 90% of custom code, and uses "time for a new team to onboard" to test whether the platform worked. (multiple orgs)

Ten-person Spawn runs a fleet of persistent agents that each own a domain, dropped PRs, and gates only irreversible external actions behind hard-coded checks.

Spawn2026-10-29 · How I Built the Most AI-Pilled Company in the World
What to borrow: Hard for large companies to copy wholesale (the author says it requires a risk appetite that lets agents break production), but borrowable pieces: write up past decisions and build a decision judge that "rules automatically when there's precedent and escalates to a human when there isn't"; put irreversible external actions behind hard-coded gates; have agents consume production error and cost anomaly signals directly; regularly delete constraints added for older models.
What they did · verbatim numbers · caveats

Ten-person Spawn does most of its work with a "fleet": persistent agents with names, memory, and real system access, each owning a piece of the company (engine, finance, bug triage, deployment), plus many helper agents they spawn. They dropped PRs in favor of "change first, coordinate after" (stigmergy), tolerating brief inconsistency but reconciling hourly. The full test suite runs against the latest code every 15 minutes. Irreversible external actions go through hard-coded gates. Agents read production errors, user reports, and spend anomalies directly. "No release in an hour" is a SEV-0. When the bottleneck became the founders' "taste decisions," they distilled two years of call transcripts and decisions into principles and built an AI judge that automatically rules on questions with precedent. Human work became "saying no," deleting outdated structure, and setting 10-100x goals.

  • We spent $3.1 million on tokens last month, which makes us one of Anthropic's biggest customers.
  • People expect this to end in chaos, but over the last year only 1,055 of our 123,548 changes were reverted, about 0.9%.
  • About 60% of our work starts from a production error, a Savi bug report (Savi is the AI you build games with in Spawn), something odd in our logs, or a feature request.
  • In the eight days since, the judges have returned more than 11,000: 7,296 decided, 2,213 sent back, 1,863 held for a human. In that time I gave my own word 27 times.
  • The week before the judges, the fleet landed about 1,000 changes a day. On October 10 it landed 7,250.
Basis: "Substantial change" is the author's own definition; token spend is about 30x payroll (author's words); the 0.9% revert rate is a full-year total.

Amazon's level guidelines name no AI tools and anchor on outcomes; input metrics like tokens are for diagnosis only.

Amazon2026-10-27 · An update from Amazon engineering leadership on the role of the developer in the age of AI
What to borrow: The most direct template for large non-lab companies: anchor level/performance criteria on outcomes, not AI usage; use input metrics for diagnosis, not as targets; use P10/P90 percentiles rather than averages to find problem teams; require leaders to have hands-on experience and be able to articulate the limits; allow and encourage engineers to document and share cases where "AI doesn't apply."
What they did · verbatim numbers · caveats

Amazon's senior principal/distinguished engineer community revised the SDE role guidelines and sent an all-hands memo. They deliberately kept specific AI tools and practices out of level criteria (so they won't be stale in a year) and anchored instead on "outcomes for customers, the business, and the team." They acknowledge using input/throughput metrics such as token usage, AI tool adoption, and code change and review counts, and state plainly that treating these as targets leads to tokenmaxxing and LOC bloat. Technical and management leaders must have used these tools hands-on in production work and be able to name the tasks where they helped and where they didn't. "AI can't help with your work" is defined as a finding, not a failure.

Microsoft Digital found individual speedups didn't add up to team speedups, so it cut handoffs with a team "constitution" and spec-driven development.

Microsoft2026-09-03 · Engineering the Frontier Firm: Sharing our AI-native approach to software development
What to borrow: A valuable negative lesson for TLMs: pushing tools alone only raises individual speed; team throughput requires changing process (fewer handoffs, specs that preserve intent). Borrow the combination of a team constitution, small-grained specs, PM-owned specs, and leadership-mandated adoption. Prerequisites: PMs and architects willing to take on new responsibilities, and specs versioned in the same repo as the code.
What they did · verbatim numbers · caveats

A retrospective on AI-native engineering at Microsoft Digital (the company's IT organization). At first everyone experimented on their own; individual developer productivity rose but team productivity did not, "a hard lesson for us," because the traditional SDLC is designed around human handoffs and every handoff loses intent. They also hit the pitfalls of vibe coding. They moved to spec-driven development (SDD): the team first jointly writes a "constitution" (architecture principles, governance, security standards), and a versioned, continuously updated spec is the single source of truth. Role changes: engineering leaders reward upfront clarification and make SDD the standard rather than optional; developers spend more time refining requirements, finding edge cases, and validating AI output; PMs own the spec; architects maintain the constitution's guardrails.

Caveats: Experience from an IT organization (internal systems), not product engineering; the post also promotes GitHub Spec Kit; no supporting data.

Anthropic's preconditions for agents as teammates: information public by default, one shared roster for humans and agents, and autonomy expanded as reliability is proven.

Anthropic2026-06-24 · Building effective human-agent teams
What to borrow: Directly actionable for TLMs: write down the team's "humans + agents roster" and responsibilities; make information public by default, with a few clear security boundaries instead of per-doc grants; start with human review and expand autonomy by task type, recording which task categories agents are trusted with; use the doer-verifier structure; cap agents' daily output so humans can review it effectively. Requires security/compliance that allows broad internal information access for agents, which at many large companies means first changing data classification policy.
What they did · verbatim numbers · caveats

For several months Anthropic has run "multiplayer agents" in Slack internally (with their own credentials, persistent memory, and broad information access), and distilled four team norms. 1) Work in the open: open up information within a few workspace-level security boundaries, make new channels public by default, and make sure decisions land in channels, docs, or meeting notes, because agents can only see what's written down. 2) Humans and agents share one roster, each with a clear role and tools (e.g. a data analysis agent, a design-system agent, a release manager agent), with agent roles defined in skill files, so people don't each run a pile of private AIs that duplicate work and fragment context. 3) Humans set a written north star and designate which agents may proactively propose new work. 4) Expand autonomy based on proven reliability: start with humans reviewing every decision and designing verification checklists, use a doer-verifier two-agent structure, have agents write a weekly "lessons and mistakes" report, widen autonomy by task type, and have agents treat human attention as scarce (batch questions, cap pending reviews per person).

Anthropic2026-06-03 · Running an AI-native engineering org
What to borrow: A practical checklist for TLMs: replace long roadmaps with just-in-time planning; change "ask the author" to "ask the agent first, then ask whether it can be automated"; do human review by domain expertise rather than on everything; have managers work as ICs first; give the team explicit license to cut process; use onboarding ramp time / PR cycle time / AI-assisted commit share as adoption metrics (while watching out for focusing only on throughput). What they did · verbatim numbers · caveats

The engineering director for Claude Code/Cowork (writeup of a Code w/ Claude SF 2026 talk) describes the norms the team rewrote once agentic coding became the default.
